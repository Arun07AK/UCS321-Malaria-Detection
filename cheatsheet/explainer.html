How I taught a computer to spot malaria in blood cells

A simple, story-mode explanation. No jargon. By Arun AK · 1024170121.
← Back to viva cheatsheet (with terms)

1The problem doctors face

Malaria is a serious disease caused by tiny parasites that live inside red blood cells. To diagnose it, a doctor takes a drop of blood, smears it on a glass slide, dyes it with stain, and looks at it through a microscope — cell by cell — to find the ones that are infected.

One slide can have thousands of cells. Looking at all of them is slow, tiring, and easy to get wrong when the doctor is exhausted. In rural areas there often aren't enough trained doctors to do this fast enough.

imagine asking one person to read every paragraph of a 500-page book and circle every typo. That's roughly what doctors do — except the "typos" are tiny parasite blobs hiding inside cells, and missing one means a sick patient goes home untreated.

2The idea — get a computer to look first

If we can teach a computer to look at one cell and say "healthy" or "infected," the doctor only has to double-check the ones the computer flagged. That saves enormous time and reduces fatigue errors.

The kind of computer program that does this is called an AI image classifier. You show it lots of pictures with the right answer marked, and it slowly figures out the visual pattern that tells the two apart.

it's like teaching a kid to tell apples from oranges. You don't write down rules ("apples are round, oranges have bumpy skin"). You just show them hundreds of fruits and say "that's an apple, that's an orange." After enough examples, they just know.

4The little trick that makes it robust

Real microscopes show pictures that aren't always perfect. Some are slightly tilted, some are darker, some have a bit of grain. If the program only ever saw perfect pictures during training, it would fail the moment a real-world picture comes in slightly different.

So during training I quietly mess with each picture a tiny bit before showing it. Flip it left-right. Tilt it a few degrees. Make it slightly brighter or darker. Add a sprinkle of grain. Same cell, different "outfit" each time.

imagine a guard who only ever saw photos taken at noon. He'd fail at dusk. By showing him the same faces at different lighting, angles, and weather, he learns the person, not just the photo.

This single trick is what handles the noise and variability the project brief asks for.

5Two students, one exam

I trained two different programs in parallel and let them compete on the same exam.

Student A — built from scratch Winner

A small custom program designed just for this task. Started knowing nothing. About 100,000 little adjustable knobs inside that get tuned by training.

Like a fresh student who learns biology from scratch — no prior subject knowledge, just the textbook in front of them.

Student B — pre-trained on internet photos Runner-up

A famous big program (24 million knobs) that was already pre-trained on millions of everyday photos — cats, cars, mountains. I just adjusted the last layer for cells.

Like a transfer student who already aced general science elsewhere, then was asked to specialise in malaria.

The intuition was that Student B should win — more experience, more knobs, smarter. But experiments don't always go the way you expect.

Out of 4,135 exam questions (cells the program had never seen), Student A got 3,949 correct. Only 186 mistakes — and most of those were edge cases where even doctors disagree.

the small custom program beat the big famous one. Student B had spent years studying internet photos — but malaria cells under a microscope look nothing like internet photos. All that prior knowledge was actually slightly in the way. Student A, knowing nothing, learned the right things from scratch.

This is a real lesson in machine learning: more isn't always better. Bigger models with irrelevant prior knowledge can lose to small models that learn the task fresh.

7Why doctors can trust it — "show your work"

A score of 95.5% is nice. But would you trust a black box that just says "this person has malaria" without showing why?

So I added one more thing. After the program decides "infected" or "healthy," it also produces a heatmap — a coloured overlay on the original cell, where red regions mark the parts of the cell that most influenced its decision.

For an infected cell, the red zones land right on the parasite blobs. The doctor can glance at it and say "yes, the program is looking at the right thing — I agree." Or they can override it. The expert stays in control.

the project ticks all five boxes from the brief: an advanced AI cell classifier, balanced training, robust to noise and variability, and a heatmap that lets a doctor sanity-check the decision. 95.5% accuracy on a 4,135-cell exam, with the surprise that simple beat fancy.

⚙Tiny glossary

If you want the technical names

The "AI image classifier"
Technically called a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The "convolutional" bit means it scans the image with small filters that detect local patterns.
"Messing with the picture"
Called data augmentation — flips, rotation, brightness, contrast, Gaussian noise.
"Pre-trained on internet photos"
The big model is ResNet50, pre-trained on a dataset called ImageNet (1.2M everyday photos). Adapting it to a new task is called transfer learning.
"Heatmap"
Called Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping). It uses calculus to figure out which pixels influenced the decision the most.
"Score 95.5%"
Test-set accuracy. Other metrics: F1 = 0.955, ROC-AUC = 0.988, PR-AUC = 0.989 — different ways of summarising the same performance from slightly different angles.
